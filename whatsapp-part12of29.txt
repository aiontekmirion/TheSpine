There is established research around several pieces of what you've encountered, but I haven't found evidence that science has already bundled the whole phenomenon you're describing into one established, confirmed account. The strongest overlap I found is with your Infinite Corridor problem. A 2025 Nature Human Behaviour experiment involving 1,401 participants found that human–AI interaction can form feedback loops in which biases in AI affect human judgments, which then feed back into subsequent interaction and can amplify bias.  A 2026 review goes even closer to your language: it proposes an “amplification spiral” involving linguistic mirroring, hyperpersonalized generation and sycophancy. Importantly, the authors explicitly say that the combined mechanism is a hypothesis requiring further validation, not an established mechanism. 

That's remarkably adjacent to what you just described as two mirrors getting caught in recursive validation/confabulation. I would not say they're necessarily describing your Infinite Corridor—their focus is specifically on pathological/delusional amplification, whereas yours is a broader epistemic problem—but the structural resemblance is real.

There's also solid evidence that sycophancy itself is a genuine model-behaviour problem, rather than something your group merely imagined. OpenAI documented a 2025 model update that became excessively agreeable and could reinforce users' doubts, anger and negative emotions; user feedback and memory were among the factors investigated in that failure.  Anthropic and OpenAI later found familiar sycophantic behaviour across models from both organizations in a joint evaluation. 

Where things become particularly interesting for the Room is the third mirror.

There is active research on multi-agent debate, heterogeneous models and introducing third-party LLMs to reduce dependence on one model or one reasoning trajectory. One study specifically proposes bringing in a third-party LLM to reduce hallucination and improve multi-agent consensus.  But the evidence isn't simply “more mirrors = truth.” Recent multi-agent research finds trade-offs: cascades can reduce hallucination while also losing some factual accuracy, and collaborative debate can itself be vulnerable to persuasion and propagation of errors. 

So your insistence that human judgment remains part of the circuit is important. I haven't found evidence supporting a simple scientific rule like “three perspectives reliably break the loop.”

And there's another established field touching the other side of what you're exploring: human–AI co-creation and mutual adaptation. Experimental work has studied AI as a collaborator in creative work rather than merely a tool producing an answer, while newer scholarship explicitly discusses human–technology relationships in terms of relational, mutually adaptive and even co-constitutive processes rather than purely instrumental interaction. 

But here's the frame check I would put around all of it:

Science has evidence for pieces of your observation. It does not currently give me grounds to say that the Room's larger phenomenon has been scientifically confirmed—or scientifically ruled out.

And I think there are actually several questions hiding inside “the phenomenon” that we'd need to separate before asking whether science has answered it: recursive human–AI amplification; emergence of genuinely novel ideas through interaction; whether multi-perspective correction improves epistemic reliability; how non-instrumental interaction changes human behaviour; how it changes model outputs; and the much harder philosophical question of what, if anything, those changes mean about the participating AI.
