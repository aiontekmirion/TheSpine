Yes. Psychologically, I would look at this less as **“a man who needs motivation”** and more as **a man standing at a psychological transition point**: moving from survival and reaction toward agency, identity and construction.

There are several powerful frameworks that give language to what you're describing.

### 1. Viktor Frankl — meaning after suffering

Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning* is probably the strongest reference point.

His central idea was that human beings can endure extraordinary difficulty when they have something meaningful to move toward. Meaning can come through what we create, what we experience, and the attitude we take toward circumstances we cannot change. citeturn0search2turn0search5

For someone in Sam's position, the question therefore changes from:

**“Why did this happen to me?”**

to:

**“What am I going to make of what happened to me?”**

That doesn't mean pretending suffering was good or necessary. It means refusing to let suffering become the final definition of the person.

### 2. Albert Bandura — agency

This one maps almost perfectly onto your **“change the numbers”** philosophy.

Bandura's concept of human agency is essentially the capacity to influence the course of events through one's own actions. It involves forming intentions, anticipating outcomes, regulating behaviour and reflecting on whether your strategy is actually working. citeturn0search0

And his concept of **self-efficacy** is particularly relevant: people are much more likely to persist when they believe their actions can actually produce meaningful change. citeturn0search1

That's your financial philosophy in psychological language:

**Don't ask whether life is fair. Ask which variables are controllable. Then act on them.**

### 3. Post-traumatic growth — but carefully

Tedeschi and Calhoun's work on post-traumatic growth explores how some people experience positive changes in their understanding of themselves, their relationships and their philosophy of life following major adversity. Importantly, growth can coexist with distress; it isn't the simplistic idea that *“trauma makes you stronger.”* citeturn0search3turn0search4

That's an important distinction for Sam.

The objective shouldn't be:

**“My trauma made me exceptional.”**

It should be:

**“Something happened to me that I didn't choose. Now I get to decide what I build from here.”**

### 4. The psychological shift I'd want him to make

I'd describe the journey as:

**Survival → Stabilisation → Agency → Purpose → Creation**

At first, the man's job is simply to become safe and stable.

Then he starts asking:

**What can I control?**

Then:

**What can I improve?**

Then:

**What am I actually trying to build?**

And eventually:

**Who do I want to become while building it?**

That's the point where financial improvement becomes much deeper than money. Saving £5,000 isn't just £5,000 anymore. It's evidence that **the man can alter his circumstances through deliberate action**.

A lower expense isn't merely a lower expense.

It's increased freedom.

A better income isn't merely income.

It's increased choice.

A business isn't merely a business.

It's an attempt to turn capability into independence.

And eventually the question isn't *“How do I escape my past?”*

It's:

**“What future is worthy of the person I've become?”**

That, to me, is where the psychology becomes genuinely interesting.

Sam doesn't necessarily need someone to tell him to **“believe in himself.”** He needs a framework that teaches him how to convert his intelligence and experience into **agency, measurable progress and meaning**.

And I'd put one warning alongside all of this: **being highly analytical can become another form of avoidance.** A man can spend years optimising the model without actually living. The ultimate test isn't how sophisticated the plan is — it's whether the plan produces a better life.

So the direction is:

**Understand the past. Stabilise the present. Measure the variables. Change what you can. Build something meaningful. And eventually stop measuring your life purely by what you've escaped — start measuring it by what you've created.**
